The Elevator

You are building the software for a bank of lifts. We fix the building and the rules every lift must obey. Everything else is yours to decide: what it looks like, how it is built, and which lift answers which call.

1. The building

  • The number of floors and the number of lifts are set when the app starts. Your app must handle anything from 2 to 20 floors and 1 to 4 lifts.
  • Floors are numbered from 0 (the ground floor) upward.
  • All lifts start at floor 0 with their doors closed.

2. Outside: on every floor

  • An Up and a Down call button. The ground floor has only Up; the top floor has only Down.
  • A display showing every lift's current floor and direction.
  • A pressed call button stays lit until a lift stops at that floor heading the way the button asked.

3. Inside: in every lift

  • One button for each floor.
  • Open door and Close door buttons.
  • A display showing the current floor and direction.
  • A pressed floor button stays lit until the lift stops at that floor.

4. Time

  • Moving one floor takes 2 seconds.
  • Doors stay open for 3 seconds, then close by themselves. Close door shuts them at once. Open door while they are closing opens them again.
  • Use simulated time, not the wall clock, so a run can be sped up.

5. The rules

These must hold at every moment, in every run. We check them automatically.

1A lift never moves while its door is open.
2A lift never goes below floor 0 or above the top floor.
3Every lit button is eventually served. Nobody waits forever.
4A lift does not reverse direction while it still has stops ahead of it in the direction it is going.
5A lift stops at a floor only if someone asked it to: its own floor button, or a call at that floor in its direction.
6A call from a floor is answered by one lift, not all of them.
7A button's light goes off when, and only when, its request is served.

6. Make it a good lift

Following the rules makes a lift safe. It does not make it good. We will put your lift in a busy building and compare how well it serves people with a simple lift we built ourselves.

What "serves people well" means is for you to decide. Your README must say what you chose, how you measured it, and which lift answers which call and why.

7. Scenarios: the one fixed interface

Your app can look like anything, but it must be able to replay a scenario: a list of button presses at given times. With one command and no UI, it reads a scenario file and writes a log of what happened. This is how we test your lift.

Scenario file (JSON):

{
  "floors": 10,
  "elevators": 2,
  "events": [
    {"t": 0,  "type": "call",  "floor": 7, "dir": "down"},
    {"t": 0,  "type": "press", "elevator": 0, "floor": 3},
    {"t": 5,  "type": "call",  "floor": 2, "dir": "up"},
    {"t": 12, "type": "open",  "elevator": 1}
  ]
}

call is a button outside on a floor. press is a floor button inside a lift. open and close are the door buttons. t is in seconds of simulated time.

Log: one JSON object per line, for every arrival and every door change. dir is the direction the lift will leave in: up, down, or idle.

{"t": 6,  "elevator": 0, "floor": 3, "dir": "up",   "event": "arrive"}
{"t": 6,  "elevator": 0, "floor": 3, "dir": "up",   "event": "door_open"}
{"t": 9,  "elevator": 0, "floor": 3, "dir": "up",   "event": "door_close"}
{"t": 14, "elevator": 1, "floor": 7, "dir": "down", "event": "arrive"}

Also log every button light turning on or off. A call button is named by floor and direction; a floor button inside a lift by lift and floor:

{"t": 0,  "event": "light_on",  "button": "call",  "floor": 7, "dir": "down"}
{"t": 0,  "event": "light_on",  "button": "press", "elevator": 0, "floor": 3}
{"t": 6,  "event": "light_off", "button": "press", "elevator": 0, "floor": 3}
{"t": 14, "event": "light_off", "button": "call",  "floor": 7, "dir": "down"}

A replay ends when every light is off and every lift is idle with its door closed, or after 3600 seconds of simulated time, whichever comes first.

8. Tests: your own rule checker

Your tests are a program that reads a scenario and the log your lift wrote for it, and reports which of the seven rules broke. Nothing else. It does not look at your lift's code, only at what the lift did. That way the same checker works on any lift that writes the log, including the one you receive on Tuesday.

What each rule looks like in a log

1Between a lift's door_open and its next door_close, that lift has no arrive at another floor.
2Every floor in the log is between 0 and the top floor.
3Every light_on has a matching light_off before the log ends.
4When a lift's dir changes from up to down at a floor, no floor button in that lift is lit for a floor above it, and no call it was given above it is still waiting. The same the other way round.
5Every door_open is at a floor that lift had a lit button for, or where a call was lit in the direction it is leaving (or it was idle).
6For each call, at most one lift opens its door at that floor, in that direction, while the call is lit.
7A light_off for a floor button comes with that lift's door_open at that floor at the same time; for a call button, with some lift's door_open at that floor leaving in that direction. No light goes off any other way, and no lit button gets a second light_on.

What to commit

  • tests/check.*: the checker. Given a scenario and a log, it prints each broken rule with the time and the log line that broke it, and exits non-zero if any rule broke.
  • tests/bad-logs/: at least one hand-written log for each rule that breaks that rule and no other, with the scenario it belongs to. Your checker must catch each one. This is how you know your checker works.
  • One command, in the README, that replays every scenario in scenarios/ through your lift, runs the checker on each log, and runs the checker on every bad log. It passes only if the good logs are clean and every bad log is caught.

How we mark it

We plant a bug in a copy of your lift: we change one line so that it breaks a rule. For example, the door may open while the lift is moving. Then we run your one test command. If it fails, your tests caught the bug and you get the mark. If it still passes, your tests missed a broken lift.

We do not tell you which rule we will break. A checker that only checks rule 1 will miss most bugs.

9. What you decide

  • What the page looks like. It must be a web page. Someone watching must be able to see, at a glance, where every lift is, which way it is going, whether its door is open, and which buttons are lit.
  • The language, the libraries and how the code is organised.
  • Which lift answers which call.
  • How you use AI to build it. Log it as usual.
